Most owners know their Booking.com commission rate. Fewer have added up what it comes to over a season. This article works through one example property so you can do the same sum with your own numbers, and then looks at what you would really keep if some of those guests booked with you directly.

## How Booking.com commission works

Booking.com charges a percentage of each reservation. Your rate is set in your own agreement, and you can see it on the invoices in your extranet. Industry guides put the range at [roughly 10% to 25%, with about 15% as a typical global average](https://www.guesty.com/blog/how-much-does-booking-com-charge-hosts), depending on market and property type.

A few things push the real figure above the headline rate:

- **It applies to the whole reservation value.** The same guide notes that commission is charged on the total booking, including fees such as cleaning, not only the nightly rate.
- **Visibility programmes cost extra.** Joining Preferred Partner raises your commission in exchange for better placement; Guesty's example is a [15% base becoming 18%](https://www.guesty.com/blog/how-much-does-booking-com-charge-hosts).
- **Genius discounts reduce what you earn per night.** You give Genius members a discount and still pay commission on what they pay.

Airbnb works differently but lands in a similar place. It has been moving hosts to a single host-only fee of [15.5%](https://www.airbnb.co.uk/resources/hosting-homes/a/simplifying-airbnb-service-fees-746), replacing the older split where the host paid about 3% and the guest paid the rest.

## A worked example: 5 rooms on the south coast

This is an illustration, not data from a real property. Change the numbers to match yours.

**Assumptions:** 5 rooms, LKR 18,000 a night, 60% average occupancy over a 30-day month, every booking through Booking.com, no Genius discount, commission charged on the room rate only.

- Room-nights sold: 5 rooms × 30 nights × 60% = 90 nights
- Room revenue: 90 × LKR 18,000 = LKR 1,620,000 a month

Here is what that revenue costs at three commission rates:

| Commission rate | Per month (LKR) | Per year (LKR) |
|---|---|---|
| 15% | 243,000 | 2,916,000 |
| 18% (for example, with Preferred Partner) | 291,600 | 3,499,200 |
| 20% | 324,000 | 3,888,000 |

At 15%, this villa hands over nearly LKR 2.9 million a year. That is a staff salary, a new roof, or a lot of fresh linen.

Real occupancy is not flat across the year, of course. A south-coast property may be close to full in February and half empty in June. The sum still holds: multiply your actual OTA revenue for the last 12 months by your actual commission rate.

## What moving 30% direct is actually worth

Direct bookings are not free either. You pay a card gateway, possibly a monthly gateway fee, and your booking software. Here is the same villa with 30% of its room-nights moved to direct bookings, using PayHere for card payments.

**Extra assumptions:** every direct guest pays the full stay by foreign card through PayHere's Plus plan, which charges [2.99% plus 1.0% for foreign-currency payments, at LKR 3,990 a month](https://www.payhere.lk/fees/). That overstates the card cost, since some guests will pay by bank transfer, LankaQR or cash. Software is the StayDirect LK Basic plan at its flat monthly price.

| Item | LKR per month |
|---|---|
| Direct revenue (30% of 1,620,000) | 486,000 |
| Commission avoided at 15% | 72,900 |
| PayHere fees (3.99% of 486,000) | −19,391 |
| PayHere Plus monthly fee | −3,990 |
| StayDirect LK Basic, flat | −5,000 |
| **Kept in this example** | **44,519** |

That is roughly LKR 534,000 a year, from moving under a third of the bookings. If you chose the percentage plan instead of the flat fee, the software line would be 2.5% of direct bookings (LKR 12,150 here), which costs more at this volume but nothing in a month with no direct bookings.

One honest caveat: this assumes those 27 nights would have been sold anyway, just through Booking.com. In practice, direct bookings come mostly from people who already know you, such as returning guests, referrals and people who find you on Google. That is why the realistic goal is a mix, not leaving the OTAs.

## Keep the OTAs, change the mix

Booking.com and Airbnb are very good at one job: putting your rooms in front of people who have never heard of you. Paying them for a first-time guest from Germany is often fair. Paying them again when the same guest returns the next year, or sends their sister, is the part worth fixing.

The guests most likely to book direct are:

- **Repeat guests** who already trust you. See [moving repeat guests to direct booking](/blog/move-repeat-guests-direct) for how to do this within platform rules.
- **Referrals** from past guests, drivers and surf schools.
- **People who search your property name on Google.** A clear booking link on your Google Business Profile catches them.
- **Walk-ins and WhatsApp enquiries**, who should be able to pay a deposit on the spot rather than being sent to an OTA.

To take those bookings safely, your own calendar has to stay in step with the OTAs. [iCal sync and double bookings](/blog/ical-sync-double-bookings) explains how.

## Checklist: run the numbers for your property

- [ ] Download your last 12 Booking.com invoices from the extranet and add up the commission.
- [ ] Do the same for Airbnb using your earnings or payout report.
- [ ] Check whether you are in Preferred Partner, Genius or any visibility programme, and what each adds.
- [ ] Count how many of last year's guests were repeat guests or referrals.
- [ ] Set a direct-booking target for next season, for example 20% or 30% of room-nights.
- [ ] Price the direct costs honestly: gateway fees, monthly fees and software.
- [ ] Decide how guests will pay a deposit. Our guide to [taking deposits with PayHere and LankaQR](/blog/deposits-payhere-lankaqr) compares the options.
